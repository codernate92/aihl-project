01 / The questionWhat happens when the record cannot be trusted?

A name.
A voice.
A way home.

Protection depends on information people can trust.

A capture card tells a family where someone is held. A message provides contact with home. A record can help establish what happened in captivity.

Synthetic media can falsify each of these. It can also make genuine evidence easier to dismiss. We examine the systems that produce, handle, and distribute this information—and where safeguards are needed.

01

Is it accurate?

The record should reflect the person’s identity, location, and condition.

02

Can it reach them?

The right people need access: prisoners, families, authorities, and humanitarian intermediaries.

03

Where did it come from?

The source and history of a record need to be traceable.

02 / The frameworkMAESTRO4IHL

Look beyond

A fabricated record passes through a system. So should the analysis.

MAESTRO4IHL adapts Ken Huang’s MAESTRO framework to examine synthetic-media risks to prisoners of war. Seven layers connect model behavior, records, distribution, oversight, and the people affected.

do what?

Different actors can protect different parts of the system. This matrix sets out proposed contributions based on their capabilities. It does not replace or redistribute legal obligations.

The five functions +
Documentation
Preserve accurate records and evidence of how they were created and handled.
Detection
Identify suspected fabrications and failures in safeguards.
Escalation
Bring findings to people and institutions able to act.
Interdiction
Interrupt harmful distribution or use where the actor has authority and control.
Hardening
Improve systems and practices so the same failure is less likely to recur.

The burden does not fall on the prisoner. People in captivity and their families should not have to solve the technical problems that put them at risk.

Geneva Convention III

The Convention addresses prisoners of war in international armed conflict. The applicable rules depend on the facts and legal classification of a conflict.

Nathan Heath is a decision scientist and AI safety researcher with 14 years of experience in geopolitics and emerging technology. Matt earned his PhD at the University of Sydney, studying the history of colonialism, international law, and scientific governance. He subsequently held research fellowships at the University of Texas at Austin and the University of Sussex. In the technology industry, he led a small AI evaluation team, collaborated on research in AI ethics, safety, and governance, and advised U.S. and international public officials on AI policy.
